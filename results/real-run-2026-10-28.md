# Delphi real-model run — 2026-10-28

Three real Bedrock models answered the Delphi Trials (prophecy, facts, reckoning, lore; craft excluded). The same answers were scored two ways, and both phases were run on each scoring.

**Update (cost-aware router, same session, $0 extra).** Routing for cost, not quality, is where the real benefit shows up. With no Bedrock calls, a cost-aware router serves the same test questions **89% cheaper than Nova Pro for −0.8 quality points (lenient)**, and **74% cheaper than Llama for −1.5 (strict)**. Neither quality loss is statistically distinguishable from zero. The design's H1 test (non-inferiority within δ=2) still **fails**, because 67 test questions are too few to bound the loss: the lenient case needs about 200. See *Cost-aware router* below.

**Short version (quality router).** On these toy questions the oracle gate **fails** under both scorings: one model is nearly as good as picking the best model per question. The router is still learnable: it captures 91–95% of the small gap on held-out test, so H3 passes. But the gain over the best single model is under one quality point, and the 95% confidence interval crosses zero. Under lenient scoring the router is 31% cheaper than the best single model (Nova Pro) at equal or better quality, so H1 reads as just met. Under strict scoring the best single model (Llama) is already cheap, and the router costs 40% *more*. Total spend: **$1.18 of the $10 cap**.

## Why there are two scorings

The dry run (5 questions per domain, $0.077) showed that the native scorers were written for the terse synthetic answers:

- **reckoning:** `numeric_match` takes the *first* integer. Both Nova models show their working ("…among 39 heirs…") and end with `\boxed{16}`, so they score 0 on answers that are right. Llama replies with just "16".
- **lore:** token F1 against a 2–4-word reference, with punctuation kept, penalises full sentences. "The Oracle sits on Mount Parnassus." scores 25.
- **facts:** worked in practice. The letter regex would let the article "a" count as **A**, but no dry-run score was flipped by it.

**Strict** is the pipeline's own `score_native`, unchanged and comparable with the synthetic run. **Lenient** re-reads the same cached answers:
- MC: the stated letter (leading, "answer is X", or "X)"), else the single option named.
- Math: the last number, preferring one after "answer is".
- Lore: recall of the reference's content words.

Prophecy uses the judge's score under both. No extra Bedrock calls were made for lenient.

## Nested or non-nested, and who wins what

The errors are **largely nested**.

- **Facts:** no question in any scoring where another model beats the best single model; Pro and Llama score 100.
- **Reckoning:** strict is decided by answer format alone (Llama wins all 80). Lenient is a three-way tie at 100: every model can do this arithmetic.
- **The only non-nested domains are prophecy and lore.** Prophecy is mostly Nova Pro: of the 31 questions with a unique winner, Pro wins 20, Lite 9 and Llama 2. Lore under lenient scoring is mostly Pro (8 unique wins against Llama's 4).
- **How often the best single model is beaten:**
  - strict (Llama): 62 of 300 questions (43 prophecy, 19 lore)
  - lenient (Nova Pro): 20 of 300 questions (13 prophecy, 7 lore)
- **Pairwise, lenient:** Nova Pro beats Llama on 46 questions and loses on 11, and beats Lite 50 to 14. That is close to a single dominant model.

**Domain winners:**
- strict: prophecy goes to Nova Pro; facts is a Pro/Llama tie; reckoning goes to Llama; lore goes to Llama.
- lenient: prophecy goes to Nova Pro; facts is a Pro/Llama tie; reckoning is a three-way tie; lore goes to Nova Pro (narrowly over Llama).

## What this says about H1, H2 and H3

These are 300 small toy questions, four of them domains, with 67 test questions. Read everything below as a demonstration of the method on real models, not as evidence about a real workload.

- **H2 (quality dominance): not supported.** The gate fails under both scorings (gap 1.2–2.3, threshold 3.0). By the pipeline's own rule this pool should pivot to H1 or be enlarged. On these questions one model (Llama on strict, Nova Pro on lenient) is close to the oracle, so there is little quality for routing to gain. The router's +0.75 to +0.91 points over the best single model are not significant: both CIs include zero.
- **H3 (learnability): passes, with a caveat.** The router captures 0.95 (strict) and 0.91 (lenient) of the oracle-minus-random gap on held-out test, against a 0.60 bar. But the gap it captures is small: oracle − random is 20.6 points strict and only 4.9 lenient. The routing signal here is mostly "which domain is this", which the toy prompts make easy to see. The capture ratio passes; the absolute gain is small.
- **H1 (same quality, ≥30% cheaper): depends on the scoring.**
  - *Lenient:* the router matches or beats Nova Pro (91.84 vs 91.09) at 31.0% lower cost. That clears the bar, but only just; on 67 questions a few routing changes could move it either way.
  - *Strict:* the best single model, Llama, is already the second-cheapest. The router is +0.91 better but costs 40% more, so H1 fails.
  - *Without a router:* under lenient scoring on test, Llama alone is 69% cheaper than Nova Pro for −1.9 points, and Nova Lite is 94% cheaper for −8.0. On a pool this nested, a single cheaper model may be the honest H1 answer.
- **The scorer matters as much as the router.** Strict scoring turns a formatting habit (Nova's worked answers) into a 100-point gap on reckoning. That flips the best single model, the router's preferred model and the H1 verdict. A real run needs answer extraction (or a uniform format instruction) that does not reward one model family's style.

## Cost-aware router (H1)

`scripts/run_phase2_costaware_real.py` runs offline from the Phase 1 answer cache (`data/real_run/cells.jsonl`) and makes no Bedrock calls; a missing cell is an error.

**Method**
- Same three router families as Phase 2, trained the same way to predict each model's quality.
- The decision changes: each query goes to argmax of predicted quality − λ × (that model's mean train cost / $0.0001), with λ swept from 0 to 100.
- **Selection rule, fixed before test:** among (family, λ) pairs whose *validation* quality is at least the best single model's validation quality, take the cheapest on validation. The best single model is chosen on train. This is stricter than H1's margin, on purpose.
- Test is scored once with the selected pair, then put through the design's own H1 test, `delphi.eval.stats.non_inferiority`: one-sided 97.5% lower bound > −2, and cost ≤ 0.70× the best single model.

| held-out test (67) | strict | lenient |
|---|---|---|
| selected (on validation) | linear, λ=15 | knn, λ=2 |
| best single model | Llama 82.70, $0.000116 | Nova Pro 91.09, $0.000377 |
| **cost-aware router** | **81.20, $0.000031** | **90.30, $0.000041** |
| quality vs best single | −1.49 (95% CI −5.25 to +1.05) | −0.80 (95% CI −2.84 to +1.44) |
| **cost saving** | **73.6%** (CI 66.0–78.8%) | **89.1%** (CI 85.7–91.6%) |
| H1 cost gate (≤ 0.70×) | pass (0.264) | pass (0.109) |
| H1 non-inferiority (lower bound > −2) | **fail** (−5.25) | **fail** (−2.84) |
| router mix | Lite 41, Llama 26 | Lite 50, Llama 14, Pro 3 |
| cheapest-correct oracle (ceiling) | 84.67, $0.000076 | 92.29, $0.000076 |

The router's cost is below the "ceiling" oracle's because it sends some questions to Lite that Lite gets wrong; the oracle never does.

**Where the router saves money**

*Lenient:*
- Reckoning goes entirely to Lite (15/15, no loss).
- Prophecy goes entirely to Lite (18/18). This is the only domain with a real loss: −3.9 points, because the judge rates Lite's prophecies slightly lower.
- Facts is split between Lite (12) and Llama (10), with no loss.
- Lore is mixed, +1.4 points.
- Overall, 11 of 67 test answers differ from Nova Pro's: 8 worse, 3 better.

*Strict:*
- Reckoning stays with Llama (15/15), since only Llama passes the strict format.
- Facts and prophecy shift mostly to Lite.
- The −1.49 is mostly one facts question Lite got wrong (−4.55 across 22 questions).
- 19 of 67 answers differ: 10 worse, 9 better.

**Why H1 fails: too little data, not a large loss.** The per-question differences are all-or-nothing on most domains, so their spread is wide (sd 8.8 lenient, 13.5 strict). If the observed mean and spread hold, the lower bound would clear −2 at about **200 test questions (lenient)**. Strict would need about 2,700, because its mean loss of −1.5 is close to the margin. A larger run of the same design (more question variety, roughly 1,000 questions) would cost an estimated $3–5, mostly the judge.

**What not to do:** loosen the selection rule, or pick λ from the test frontier the log prints. The frontier is descriptive only. The selection was fixed before test was scored, and changing it now would be choosing the result.

**Reading for the blog:** on real Bedrock models these toy questions show no quality win from routing (H2), but a large, learnable cost win. The router sends easy questions to the cheapest model: 74–89% cheaper, with a quality cost under 1.5 points that isn't distinguishable from zero. The pre-registered non-inferiority test isn't met yet; it needs a test set about three times larger.
